I think these four questions converge on one project: use Exegenius as a place to develop, test and accumulate the research creativity of increasingly capable models, while improving the software itself.

The paper provides a possible conceptual reframing. The mathematical discoveries you quoted suggest that frontier models may be starting to do more than optimise within established approaches: they may invent unexpected conceptual moves. TasteVal suggests that the ability to choose and interpret experiments is also improving. The opportunity is to build a development process that can exploit both capabilities rather than treating each model as a better coding assistant.

I'd make three changes to what we've discussed so far.

## 1. Change the objective of the article-inspired task

Don't ask Claude simply to find a weakness and fix it. Ask it to investigate whether our understanding of the problem is itself inadequate, then make a validated intervention.

The deliverable remains a concrete improvement to Exegenius. But the model is allowed to challenge the existing architecture, invent a different approach, or conclude that the most valuable intervention is an experiment that tests a radically different formulation.

## 2. Turn the task into a repeatable discovery benchmark

Preserve each episode as a reusable case containing:

- The problem, relevant system state and evidence available at the time.
- The model's initial diagnosis and alternative approaches.
- Its proposed conceptual move and why it expects that move to help.
- The experiment, implementation and independently checked outcome.
- What was novel, what actually worked, what failed and what was learned.

Run future models against a growing set of historical and fresh problems. Compare conceptual novelty, research judgment and validated impact separately. This lets us discover whether newer models genuinely produce better ideas, not just more persuasive proposals.

## 3. Make the process compound

The long-term asset isn't the prompt or even the benchmark. It's the accumulated body of evidence about how to improve Exegenius, which future models can use to make better decisions.

A new model should be able to inspect previous failures, recognise an untested possibility, devise a discriminating experiment, implement a successful change and leave the next model better equipped to continue. That is a plausible way to harness increasing research creativity; whether it produces a meaningful recursive improvement loop remains an empirical question.

## What I would do next

I would give the current article-inspired task one final revision to incorporate this discovery objective, then let the fresh Claude Code session run. No more conceptual discussion before it produces an outcome.

The first session should deliver a tested intervention and a structured record of the discovery process. That record becomes the prototype for the repeatable benchmark. Once it exists, we can use it to assess the new Fable model against the current baseline.

That gives us a concrete path from the article, through a software intervention, towards a process that can take increasing advantage of future models' ability to discover what we haven't thought to ask for yet.
