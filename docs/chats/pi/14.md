That adds another substantial connection: the Craftseer demo wasn't just a sequence of show steps, but a short text organised by acts, with turns of interaction directed towards outcomes.

The important feature is that the acts weren't necessarily governed by an explicit specification of what would count as success. The interaction continued through attempts and revisions until some tacit condition of satisfaction had been met. You could recognise that the exchange had arrived somewhere, even though the stopping condition hadn't been stated in advance.

That suggests three aspects of Craftseer worth distinguishing:

- Show steps: successive moves that make the language game intelligible, including spelling out its rules.
- Act structure: larger movements in the exchange, each directed towards achieving some outcome.
- Implicit satisfaction conditions: the criteria by which an attempt is judged sufficient to move on, even when those criteria haven't been formalised.

The second and third points may be especially relevant to Exegenius. A source-processing agent shouldn't necessarily be understood as executing a fully specified plan from beginning to end. It may have to work iteratively towards an outcome, judge whether its current representation is adequate, and revise or continue when it isn't.

There's an interesting contrast here with the proof-plan model. A proof plan can specify the intended result and the inferential steps towards it, but it doesn't automatically specify how an agent knows that its current analysis is good enough, particularly when the task is reconstructive and the source doesn't provide a definitive success criterion.

And the brevity of the Craftseer text matters. Even in a short exchange, a great deal of organisation can be distributed across turns and acts. Length alone doesn't determine structural complexity; a short dialogue can have a rich process structure, while a long philosophical book may have a less explicit global plan.

I think this gives you a useful distinction for the journal note:

The representation of a source's structure — its content, narrative, argument and show steps — is one problem. The organisation of the process by which an agent constructs and evaluates that representation is another. Craftseer may have brought both into view at once.

The latter also suggests that the non-proof-plan IR might be more than a descriptive format for documents. It could provide intermediate representations that an agent uses while working towards a task whose satisfaction conditions are only partly explicit. The IR describes what has been understood so far; the agent's continuing work depends on judging whether that understanding is sufficient for its current purpose.

I'd keep that as a possible design implication rather than treating it as already established. The striking observation is that the same little demo seems to have exposed both a structure of explanation and a structure of purposeful, iterative activity.
