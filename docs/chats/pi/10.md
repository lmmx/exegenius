I think there is a connection here, but perhaps it isn't that the Craftseer experiment and Exegenius are doing the same kind of analysis. It is that both involve recovering the structure of an activity from a text, and then representing that structure in a different form.

There are a few things that might be getting tangled together.

## 1. The chat is a performance, not just a text

In Craftseer, the user/LLM exchange is an instance of a language game. Its meaning depends on what the participants are doing with their turns: responding to one another, following rules, recognising a move as appropriate, and so on.

When you converted the chat into a narrative, you weren't merely summarising its contents. You were trying to make the capacity demonstrated by the interaction intelligible in another medium.

The narrative is a representation of the performance. It needs to preserve what makes the interaction work, even though it no longer takes the form of that interaction.

That seems like a significant distinction. A summary might tell you what was said; a narrative of the performance should let you understand what was accomplished by saying it.

## 2. The literary document presents a different problem

The mathematical paper in `openai-nlogn-wiki` is comparatively amenable to reconstruction as an argument plan. It has claims to establish, intermediate results, dependencies and inferential steps. Its exposition can be modelled in terms of how it advances a proof.

But even a mathematical paper is not simply a proof. It also introduces notation, motivates choices, explains significance, and guides the reader through the material.

And Philosophical Investigations makes the problem more pronounced: the reader has to work out what the remarks are doing, how they relate to earlier remarks, and how the investigation develops without necessarily converging on a single theorem-like result.

So you have at least three different objects:

- A performance: a language game enacted through a sequence of conversational moves.
- A literary or philosophical work: an exposition whose structure must be recovered from the text.
- A mathematical argument: a structured sequence of results and inferences, potentially decomposable into a proof plan.

These aren't mutually exclusive categories, but they foreground different kinds of structure.

## 3. The interesting connection may be the change of representation

Here's where I think your Craftseer experiment becomes relevant to the proposed `content / narrative / argument` IR.

### Craftseer

Conversational performance

A sequence of user/LLM turns demonstrates a language game.

Narrative reconstruction

The interaction is represented in a form that makes its structure and demonstrated capacity intelligible.

### Exegenius

Written source

A document contains concepts, exposition, examples and argumentative moves.

Part-localised reconstruction

Content, narrative and argument are extracted so that local explanations can retain their relationship to the whole.

Global plan, where appropriate

Local structures may be composed into a larger plan if the source supports one.

In both cases, the transformation has to preserve something that would be lost by a straightforward summary.

For Craftseer, that is the organisation of the interaction as a performance. For Exegenius, it is the organisation of the source as an act of exposition and reasoning.

The important point is that the representation you want depends on what kind of thing the source is doing, not merely on its length or subject matter.

## 4. Is the orality distinction useful?

Yes, but I would treat it as a secondary observation rather than the main connection.

A conversational exchange is turn-organised, locally responsive and dependent on shared context. In that sense, it has affinities with orality, although a recorded LLM chat is not literally oral speech. Its short turns make the sequence of moves particularly visible.

A written philosophical work, by contrast, can sustain long-range references and relationships without explicitly restating them at every point. Its reader may need to reconstruct those relationships across considerable distances.

This suggests a difference in the distribution of structure:

- In a dialogue, much of the structure is visible in the sequence of moves and their immediate responses.
- In a book, some of the structure may be distributed across passages whose relationship is not explicitly signalled.
- In a proof, dependencies can often be stated more directly, although the author's explanatory choices still matter.

Your interest in the narrative of a language game may therefore have helped expose a problem that also appears in document analysis: how do you represent the organisation of something when that organisation is not reducible to its propositional content?

I wouldn't yet claim that orality is the theoretical bridge. The more general issue of representing an activity through a different textual form seems stronger.
