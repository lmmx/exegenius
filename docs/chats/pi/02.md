### 1. A prerequisite graph is not an argument

Your initial wiki models concepts as pages with prerequisite relationships. That answers a question like:

> What does a reader need to know before they can understand this concept?

But it doesn't answer the more interesting question:

> Why does the author take this particular route from the starting assumptions to the main result?

The October 11 proof-plan entry makes this distinction explicit, drawing on Hamami and Morris's account of understanding mathematical proofs. Understanding requires reconstructing not just the individual inferences, but the author's plan for connecting them.&#x20;

### 2. The plan distinguishes two kinds of reasoning

This is the part of your third post that most needs a plain-English explanation.

- Show: A goal that cannot be accomplished in one direct step, so it must be broken down into subordinate goals. For example, to explain why a philosophical picture is misleading, you might first need to expose its assumptions, then examine examples that undermine them.
- Infer: A step that can be carried out directly, given what has already been established. In a mathematical proof, this might be a particular deduction. In philosophical analysis, the analogue might be a conclusion drawn from an example or an objection supported by a passage.

These are not two types of content, nor simply two kinds of sentences. They are two kinds of proving intention in the framework you are borrowing: one specifies what needs to be shown, and the other specifies a step that directly advances the argument.&#x20;

### 3. You care about the connections that make the whole work intelligible

Your aha moments idea is more specific than finding the key sentence on each page.

A sentence might be important because it reconnects an argument's local details with its overall purpose. The reader finally sees why a distinction introduced much earlier mattered, or why an apparently incidental example was doing real work.

The journal distinguishes three things that can coincide but need not:

- Key step: the claim that does the decisive work.
- Payoff: the explanation of why that work matters.
- Reconnection: the point at which an earlier idea becomes intelligible in relation to the larger goal.

Your aim is to identify these connections and potentially test their importance by removing a candidate sentence or page and checking whether a simulated reader can still reconstruct the relevant relationships.&#x20;

## The important caveat: this is an empirical question, not just a design idea

The recent development journal adds another useful dimension to your original question about the size limit.

Your experiments are testing how reliably models can reconstruct the reasoning in a technical source, whether asking for deeper derivations improves the plan, and whether that improvement survives checks for errors. And it suggests a useful way to frame your original question:

- Context-window limit: How much source material can the model work with at once?
- Structural limit: How much of the source's global organisation can it reconstruct?
- Fidelity limit: How much can it reconstruct without inventing connections or introducing errors?
- Evaluation limit: How can we tell whether the plan is actually right, rather than merely plausible?

A work already represented in training might benefit from the model's prior knowledge, but that knowledge could also mask gaps in the reconstruction. Your experiments with simulated readers, source quotations and checks for unsupported reasoning address different parts of that problem.

## What I would post next

I'd avoid trying to explain the whole architecture in another instalment. Your friend has asked what you mean, so the best next post is probably a compact statement of the intellectual ambition, followed by a concrete explanation of why Wittgenstein is a difficult test case.

The distinction I’m trying to get at is between a dependency graph of concepts and a reconstruction of an author’s reasoning.

A concept wiki can tell you what depends on what. But that doesn’t necessarily tell you *why* the author takes a particular route through the material, what each step is doing, or where several apparently separate threads come together.

I’ve been experimenting with making that second structure explicit. The argument is represented as a tree of intentions: some steps set out what needs to be shown and decompose it into subgoals; others are direct inferential steps. Each step has a method, a motivation, dependencies, and a place in the source. The resulting plan can then guide the writing of individual wiki pages.

The interesting problem is that a source can be locally intelligible while its global argument remains obscure. I also want to identify the passages that reconnect distant parts of the argument — the points where the significance of an earlier idea becomes apparent — without collapsing the whole wiki into one enormous exposition.

That’s why I thought of *Philosophical Investigations*. It’s a difficult test of whether this machinery generalises beyond conventional mathematical proofs: can you reconstruct a coherent structure of philosophical inquiry without pretending that the book is a linear deduction?

The size limit is only one part of the question. The harder one is how to tell whether the structure an LLM reconstructs is genuinely in the work, rather than a plausible structure it has supplied itself.
