I think the right modification is to treat Philosophical Investigations not as a bigger version of the technical paper, but as a source whose structure is liable to be damaged by the existing decomposition strategy.

The central architectural issue is that the system commits to a representation of the source before it has necessarily understood what kind of representation the source needs. Once that representation becomes a concept graph, prerequisite ordering, argument tree, and set of pages, downstream tasks inherit its assumptions. More writing capacity doesn't necessarily fix that.

I'd approach this by changing how Exegenius builds and refines its intermediate representation of a work. Drafts could help, but not merely as an extra writing pass. They'd be useful if they let the system discover and revise the structure before committing to it.

## 1. Give the work a provisional global representation before extracting the full graph

For a long work, I'd introduce an intermediate stage between raw source and the existing extraction.

Instead of immediately asking for a definitive concept graph, ask for a provisional structural map that records:

- The source's apparent large-scale movements and local sequences.
- The kinds of intellectual activity occurring: argument, example, objection, analogy, distinction, thought experiment, clarification, and so on.
- Recurring terms and concepts, including where their meaning appears to shift.
- Explicit cross-references, dependencies, tensions, and unresolved questions.
- Places where the structure is uncertain or admits competing readings.

This is not a summary intended for the final reader. It is a working hypothesis about how the source is organised.

For PI, that distinction matters. A sequence of numbered remarks isn't necessarily a sequence of premises leading to a conclusion. An example might undermine a temptation rather than establish a proposition. A later remark might transform the significance of an earlier one without simply depending on it.

The provisional map gives extraction a better starting point, while remaining revisable. Crucially, it should preserve source locations so that every proposed relationship can be traced back to the text.

## 2. Use Wittgenstein's remarks as the atomic source units

For this particular book, I would make the existing extraction source-aware in a more literal way: first establish stable, addressable units corresponding to the numbered remarks, rather than letting the model invent its own initial granularity.

The book contains 693 numbered sections in Part I, but the numbering doesn't give you a ready-made outline. The remarks can form short local chains, then jump to another subject; scholarship explicitly identifies both small-scale argumentative patterns and larger trains of thought across the work.&#x20;

I'd use three levels:

Level A — Source remarks

Immutable source text, stable identifiers, paragraph boundaries, original ordering, and exact quotation spans. No interpretive rewrite replaces the source.

Level B — Local dossiers

A small group of adjacent remarks, with a draft account of what each is doing, how the remarks relate, and what remains unclear. Every claim points back to one or more source spans.

Level C — Global map

Cross-dossier connections, recurring problems, changes in terminology, returns to earlier examples, and competing accounts of the work's larger organisation.

The key is that a dossier is not a summary that gets to replace its constituent remarks. It's a layer of interpretation over stable source units.

This also makes source decomposition deterministic. The model doesn't have to decide where the book's paragraphs are, or reconstruct source locations from memory. It can focus on what the remarks are doing.

## 3. Yes, use drafts — but use them to discover structure

I would introduce drafts at two distinct stages.

First, local drafts. For each group of neighbouring remarks, produce a provisional interpretation with explicit fields:

- What happens in each remark?
- What question, temptation, or claim is being considered?
- Does the next remark support, challenge, qualify, illustrate, or redirect the previous one?
- Which connections are explicit in the text, and which are proposed by the interpreter?
- What evidence would make this account wrong?

Second, a global draft. Once the local drafts exist, synthesise them into a provisional account of the book's movements and recurring concerns. This draft should identify where local accounts appear to conflict, where the same concept seems to do different work, and where the global account requires an interpretation not established by any local dossier.

Then feed those discrepancies back into the representation, rather than simply asking a writer to smooth them over.

For example, suppose a local draft treats a series of remarks as a reductio of a particular picture of meaning. A global draft might discover that this account doesn't explain why Wittgenstein returns to a similar example much later. The correct response isn't automatically to rewrite the later page so that it fits. It might mean the first interpretation was too strong, that the later passage has a different function, or that two plausible readings should remain open.

The draft is therefore a hypothesis generator and consistency constraint, not an authoritative source and not merely an intermediate version of the final prose.

## 4. Change the graph model: not everything is a prerequisite

This is probably the most important change to the machinery itself.

The current concept graph is good at representing a relation of the form “understand A before B.” But many important relationships in PI are not prerequisites.

I'd keep the existing prerequisite graph, but add a distinct graph of source-level relationships:

| Relation              | Meaning                                                 |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| `continues`           | A remark continues a local line of thought              |
| `illustrates`         | An example gives concrete form to a point               |
| `challenges`          | A remark puts pressure on a claim or picture            |
| `reframes`            | A later remark changes how an earlier one is understood |
| `returns_to`          | A recurring problem or example reappears                |
| `contrasts_with`      | Two passages are meaningfully opposed                   |
| `depends_on`          | A later step requires an earlier claim or distinction   |
| `possibly_related_to` | A plausible connection whose status remains uncertain   |

These would be proposed relationships, each with evidence and a confidence or epistemic status—not facts merely because a model produced them.

I'd also distinguish the kind of source unit from its content. For instance, a passage might be classified as an example, an objection, a question, a provisional formulation, or a therapeutic redirection. The classifications could be multiple and revisable.

Why? Because otherwise the existing pipeline risks turning every passage into a concept that must be learned, and every important relation into a dependency that must be followed. That is a reasonable fit for some technical papers, but it could misrepresent a text whose philosophical work is done through the movement between remarks.

## 5. Change parallelism: parallelise local reading, centralise global synthesis

I'd retain parallel page production, but organise the upstream work differently.

Deterministic source preparation

Stable remark IDs, source spans, original order, paragraph boundaries

Local batch A

Draft dossiers for one group of remarks

Local batch B

Draft dossiers for another group

Local batch C

Draft dossiers for another group

Local batch N

Draft dossiers for another group

Global synthesis

Resolve overlaps, compare interpretations, identify cross-references and contradictions

Targeted revision

Revise affected dossiers and their dependent pages, without rebuilding the whole work

The local batches are independent enough to parallelise. The global synthesis is not: it needs to compare their outputs and detect inconsistencies across the whole work.

I would not require every local task to receive the entire book. Instead, give it the relevant remarks, a limited surrounding window, the current global index, and explicitly selected related passages. That preserves local focus while making long-range connections discoverable.

The crucial part is that the global synthesis must be able to challenge the local drafts, not just concatenate them. And a local draft must be allowed to challenge the global synthesis in return.

## 6. Make revision incremental and preserve interpretive uncertainty

A large work should not need to be reconstructed from scratch whenever one relationship changes.

I'd give each dossier and global-map entry a version and record which source spans, relationships, and other dossiers it depends on. When an interpretation changes, the system can identify which downstream pages need reconsideration.

But I would also avoid collapsing every uncertainty into a single best answer. A dossier could carry multiple candidate interpretations:

- Textually explicit: directly stated or unmistakably marked by the source.
- Strongly supported: a reconstruction of the passage's role with substantial textual evidence.
- Interpretive: a plausible account of how the passage functions.
- Disputed or unresolved: materially different accounts remain viable.

These aren't necessarily confidence scores; they're different kinds of epistemic commitment. The system should be able to say that a proposed global structure depends on an interpretive connection, rather than silently treating it as a premise.

That is particularly important here because the published work itself resists a simple linear outline. The scholarship on PI discusses both its local argumentative patterns and the difficulty of understanding the relation between its different voices and trains of thought.&#x20;

## 7. What I would actually change in Exegenius

I'd approach this as a change to the existing architecture, not a replacement for it.

1. Add source-unit preparation. Create stable remark-level source records before semantic extraction. Preserve original ordering and exact source spans.
2. Add provisional local dossiers. Generate structured, source-linked interpretations before constructing the final concept graph and argument tree.
3. Add a global-map artefact. Capture the work's large-scale movements, cross-references, unresolved tensions, and alternative structural readings.
4. Extend the graph schema. Preserve prerequisite edges, but represent rhetorical and interpretive relationships separately.
5. Make planning consume the drafts and map. The argument planner should be able to represent sequences of examples, challenges, and reframings—not only conventional inferential steps.
6. Use dependency-aware revision. Changes propagate to affected artefacts, while unaffected local work remains intact.

I'd leave the existing writing, simulated-reader, and page-generation machinery in place initially. Those stages can consume the richer representation without requiring the entire system to be rewritten at once.

The deeper design principle is that the source should determine the representation, rather than the existing representation determining what the source is allowed to be.

And yes, drafts are central to that idea. Not because the system needs more prose passes, but because a large, difficult work needs room for its structural interpretation to develop before that interpretation becomes the fixed dependency graph from which everything else is generated.
