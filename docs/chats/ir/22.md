I think this points towards a general architecture for Exegenius, rather than a Wittgenstein-specific workaround. The important distinction is between how the source is represented during understanding and how that understanding is ultimately presented to a reader.

Those don't have to be the same representation.

## 1. One intermediate representation, multiple source-specific structures

I'd separate three things:

- Source units: the stable pieces of evidence from which we work.
- Interpretive drafts: provisional accounts of what those pieces do, how they relate, and what the source is doing overall.
- Reader-facing representation: the structure Exegenius ultimately uses to teach, explain, or reconstruct the work.

The first two could be common infrastructure across document types. The third would be adapted to the kind of work.

Source preparation

Stable units, ordering, source spans, section structure

Intermediate representation

Local drafts, source-linked relationships, global map, unresolved interpretations

### Technical paper

Definitions, assumptions, methods, results, lemmas, show/infer proof steps

### Philosophical work

Examples, objections, conceptual distinctions, recurring problems, reframings

Shared interpretive infrastructure; different downstream projections.

This would mean the OpenAI n(log n) paper could use the same general process. Its Methods section, for example, could be a natural local unit or group of units. A draft would capture not just what the methods are, but what they establish, what assumptions they depend on, and how they connect to the results.

The difference is in the kind of relations the intermediate representation discovers, not necessarily in the pipeline itself.

For a technical paper, the final projection might emphasise formal dependencies and proof-like explanation. For Philosophical Investigations, it might emphasise conceptual development, examples, challenges, and unresolved tensions. Both can benefit from local drafts and a global account before generating reader-facing pages.

## 2. I would not make the intermediate representation itself a proof tree

This is the subtle part of your question about transforming it into `show`/`infer` proof types.

I would preserve the intermediate representation as a richer, source-grounded model. Then have a downstream transformation derive the proof-like representation where that is appropriate.

For example, a technical paper might contain a chain like:

- Source-level account: the authors define a problem, introduce a construction, explain its properties, and use it to establish a result.
- Intermediate representation: the construction depends on assumptions A and B; the result depends on the construction and lemma C; the paper's experiment supplies empirical rather than deductive evidence for another claim.
- Proof-oriented projection: a `show`/`infer` tree for the mathematically inferential parts, with empirical and methodological claims represented separately.

Not every claim in a technical paper is a mathematical inference. And not every meaningful relation in a philosophical text should be forced into one.

The intermediate representation should therefore be more expressive than the target format. A projection may omit irrelevant relations, but it shouldn't destroy them in the underlying record.

One practical consequence: the transformation should preserve provenance. If a proof step is generated from a local draft, you should be able to trace it through the draft's relationships to the original source spans. If the transformation cannot justify a proof step, it should flag that gap rather than silently manufacture the missing inference.

## 3. The ordering problem is real — and I think there's a clean answer

Your concern about making the global map dependent on the local drafts is exactly right.

If the global model reads local drafts and those drafts were themselves written under the influence of an earlier global interpretation, the whole process can become self-confirming. Worse, if the local tasks run sequentially and each sees earlier outputs, then the result can depend on execution order.

I'd use parallel local drafting followed by global synthesis, with an explicit separation between the two phases.

Frozen source

Canonical source units and metadata

Local draft A

Same source policy; independent task

Local draft B

Same source policy; independent task

Local draft C

Same source policy; independent task

Global synthesis

Reads all local drafts together, identifies connections and conflicts

The local tasks needn't be blind to all context: they can see the source's original section headings, nearby remarks, and explicit cross-references. But they shouldn't see each other's evolving interpretations, nor a changing global interpretation, during the initial drafting pass.

Then the global synthesis can read the whole set of local outputs and discover relationships that no individual local task could reliably identify.

There is one caveat: if the source is large enough, the global synthesis might not be able to read every local draft in a single context. That doesn't mean you need to fall back to serial processing. You can construct the global map hierarchically: synthesise groups of local drafts in parallel, then synthesise those higher-level drafts. The global stages are sequential by level, but siblings at each level remain independent.

And because the global map is derived rather than authoritative, you can revise it without rewriting the source or erasing the original local drafts.

## 4. The global map should not be merely a summary of the local drafts

I would give it a different job.

A local draft answers:

> What is happening here, and what evidence supports that account?

The global map answers:

> What relationships become visible across the work, and what does the local evidence collectively warrant?

Those are different questions. The global synthesis should be able to identify:

- Connections that weren't visible locally.
- Similar terms used in different ways.
- Contradictions between local interpretations.
- Claims that look globally important but have weak local support.
- Gaps between the source's actual structure and the structure imposed by the drafts.
- Alternative global organisations that explain the same local material.

I'd also preserve the distinction between source relationships and interpretive relationships. Two passages might share a topic without one depending on the other. A later passage might illuminate an earlier one without proving that it was the earlier passage's intended meaning.

That gives you a representation where relationships are explicit objects with evidence and provenance, rather than invisible assumptions buried in generated prose.

## 5. The architecture I'd aim for

I would think of the drafts as a general-purpose intermediate representation, not a special mode for difficult books:

1. Segment and identify the source without interpretation.
2. Draft locally from stable source units.
3. Synthesize globally from the local drafts, without order-dependent local interaction.
4. Reconcile the local and global accounts, preserving competing interpretations where needed.
5. Project the resulting representation into the appropriate reader-facing structure: proof steps, concept pages, methods-and-results explanations, philosophical sequences, or some combination.

The important design choice is that stages 2–4 aren't simply three summaries of increasing length. They produce different kinds of structured artefacts, each with its own purpose and provenance.

That seems like a promising generalisation of Exegenius: a system that doesn't have to decide at the very beginning whether a source is best understood as a proof, a dependency graph, or a sequence of conceptual moves. It can first build a richer account of the source, and only then decide which structures to derive from it.
