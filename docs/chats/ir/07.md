I think you've found something genuinely useful here, even if you don't yet know exactly what the experiments establish. Reading through this, my main impression is that you're discovering which parts of the pipeline can be calibrated empirically, and which parts are bottlenecked by the definition of the task itself.

And the latter may be the more important discovery.

## What I think your results actually say

I'd separate the findings into four categories.

| Component               | Current evidence                                                                          | My interpretation                                                            |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Extraction and planning | Sonnet High reaches the ceiling on every tested extraction with the revised instruction   | Promising, but ceiling scores limit your ability to distinguish better plans |
| Writing                 | Opus xhigh outperforms Sonnet High on two difficult pages in the repeat experiment        | A plausible targeted upgrade, not yet a general policy                       |
| Judging                 | Pairwise comparison passes the known-better-plan tests and detects a single-step ablation | A useful measurement instrument, with remaining limitations                  |
| Fidelity auditing       | All three models miss the same source-dependent errors                                    | Strong evidence that the current task formulation is inadequate              |

The planning result is particularly interesting because your E11 comparison did not show that Opus is a better planner. That's useful negative evidence. You have a hypothesis, a preregistered decision rule, and a result that doesn't support the hypothesis.

The writer result points in the opposite direction: there may be a real capability difference on difficult pages, despite Opus's additional cost. Your W3 experiment should help establish whether the advantage extends beyond the two pages already tested.

But I'd be careful with one statistical distinction: failing to demonstrate that Opus is better does not establish that the models are equivalent. With six extraction comparisons, a nonsignificant result leaves considerable uncertainty.

## The most revealing result is A1

The fact that all three models missed the same four errors, including both generalisation errors, is the result I'd spend the most time thinking about.

You tested Haiku, Sonnet and Opus. Increasing model capability didn't resolve the failure.

That suggests a plausible explanation: the auditor is being asked to judge a page using evidence that is insufficient to expose certain errors.

Your own diagnosis of the existing checks makes this particularly clear:

- The reader audit tests whether the explanation is understandable.
- The support audit tests whether a claim is supported by its attached quotation.
- Neither necessarily tests whether the quotation itself is an adequate representation of the original paper's claim.

A page can be clear, internally consistent, and faithfully supported by a quotation while still misrepresenting the source.

For example, imagine an original paper establishes:

> Under assumptions A and B, method X achieves a particular bound.

The wiki quotes the correct bound but silently omits assumption B. The quotation is genuine. The explanation is coherent. The local claim-support check might pass. Yet the wiki has generalised the result beyond what the paper establishes.

No amount of additional reasoning effort reliably fixes a task that never requires the auditor to compare the complete claim against the relevant source conditions.

Your A2 design directly addresses this by providing source passages and planting errors that can only be detected against the original paper. I think that's a more consequential next experiment than simply trying a stronger model.

## One thing I'd change about the experimental strategy

Your experiments are becoming rigorous enough that I'd introduce an explicit distinction between three different kinds of uncertainty.

### 1. Model uncertainty

Does one model produce better results than another on the same task?

Your Sonnet–Opus writer comparisons address this.

### 2. Measurement uncertainty

Can your evaluator reliably distinguish correct from incorrect outputs?

K3 and the `ASSERTS` classifier investigation address this, but the classifier's high overall agreement masks its poor sensitivity to actual error assertions.

### 3. Task-specification uncertainty

Does the task actually require the behaviour you want?

A1's source-blind auditing is the clearest example. A2 is designed to test a more appropriate specification.

These uncertainties interact. If a judge cannot reliably distinguish two plans, a model comparison tells you little. If an auditor isn't given the source evidence needed to identify an error, comparing models doesn't answer which model is best at fidelity auditing.

This gives you a useful rule for allocating quota: resolve task-specification and measurement problems before spending heavily on model comparisons whose outcomes depend on them.

## The larger question: can the pipeline discover its own weaknesses?

Here's where I think your project becomes a grand challenge rather than merely a sophisticated documentation system.

At present, you're manually designing experiments, planting errors, specifying evaluation criteria, and interpreting the results. That is entirely sensible. But eventually, you could make the system itself responsible for proposing and testing improvements to its own reconstruction process.

For example:

1. The auditor repeatedly misses generalisation errors.
2. An analysis stage identifies that the current task lacks an explicit source-to-claim comparison.
3. The system proposes a new auditing instruction and predicts which error classes it should improve.
4. You preregister the experiment, including the success criterion and cost.
5. The system runs the experiment and evaluates the result.
6. The policy changes only if the evidence meets the specified criterion.

Your A1-to-A2 progression is already an example of this feedback loop, albeit one guided by you.

The eventual objective would be a pipeline that improves not merely by generating better text, but by discovering which of its own operations fail, why they fail, and what experiment would most efficiently resolve the uncertainty.

That is a much more interesting optimisation problem than prompt tuning alone.

## What I'd do next, in your position

My order would be:

1. Run A2 before more auditor model comparisons. Its source-aware arm tests the suspected structural failure directly. Hand-labeling its results is a good way to avoid repeating the `ASSERTS` sensitivity problem.
2. Run W3 if its cost is acceptable. You already have the work prepared, and it tests a practical policy decision. Preserve the distinction between its preregistered primary outcome and the secondary analysis of whether Sonnet actually meets the fidelity standard.
3. Keep E11 as a negative result. Don't keep rerunning the planner comparison until Opus wins. Revisit it when you have a specific reason to expect a changed result, such as a different task or a revised instruction.
4. Treat the next paper as the generalisation test. The current results calibrate configurations for this particular paper. They don't yet establish a general recipe for arbitrary coherent documents.

One additional experiment I'd eventually want is a cross-paper transfer test: calibrate on the multiplication paper, freeze the configuration, and then evaluate on a second technical work without retuning. That distinguishes a genuinely reusable pipeline from one that has been carefully optimised to its initial benchmark.

My overall reading: you don't yet know whether the entire approach generalises, but you have evidence that parts of it can be calibrated, that some model upgrades are task-dependent, and that a seemingly sensible verification step can fail for structural reasons.

That's real progress. The most valuable result may ultimately be the discovery that reliable decomposition requires a different verification architecture, not simply a more capable model.

And that brings us back to Philosophical Investigations: the decisive challenge won't be whether Exegenius can produce an elegant, navigable wiki of Wittgenstein. It will be whether it can distinguish a faithful reconstruction of his argumentative moves from a persuasive reconstruction that quietly resolves the very ambiguities his work leaves open.
