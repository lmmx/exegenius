I think your hesitation is justified, and I think there is a way through it that doesn't require retreating from the original design. The important distinction is between a document having a proof-like structure and a document being reducible to a proof. Those are not the same claim.

I also think your observation about the narrative of a passage is more substantial than it might initially sound. It suggests a missing level in Exegenius, rather than a reason to abandon the proof-plan idea.

## 1. The narrative is not an alternative to the proof plan

Consider the difference between these three descriptions of a hypothetical passage in a philosophical work:

- Content: The author discusses several cases in which a word appears to have a stable meaning.
- Narrative: The author brings apparently straightforward cases into view, then complicates them to expose a difficulty in the assumption that meaning is fixed by a single underlying feature.
- Argument: The initial cases do not establish that meaning has a single underlying feature; the complications undermine that generalisation.

These descriptions are related, but they do different work.

The content description catalogues what is there. The narrative description captures the movement of the passage: where it begins, what pressure it introduces, and what changes for the reader. The argument description reconstructs the inferential force of the material.

The narrative can make the argument easier to reconstruct because it tells you what the individual claims are doing. It can distinguish an example offered as evidence from an example intended to destabilise an assumption, or a temporary formulation from a conclusion the author endorses.

That is precisely the kind of information a list of claims and prerequisite edges can lose.

And the narrative itself is not merely a literary flourish. It can be a hypothesis about the author's argumentative intentions, grounded in the sequence and wording of the source. It should therefore retain its evidence and its uncertainty, just as the claims do.

## 2. Does this mean two different document types need two different architectures?

I would distinguish three things:

Source representation

What the document actually says, with stable references to its passages, sections, claims, examples, definitions, and other source features.

Interpretive representation

What the parts are doing, how the author's thought develops, what tensions or relationships connect them, and what inferential commitments can be reconstructed.

Proof-plan representation

The claims, dependencies, assumptions, inferential steps, and supporting material that can be organised into a proof-like account of the work.

I would make these layers part of the same architecture, rather than make one architecture for philosophy and another for technical papers.

The source representation shouldn't assume that every passage is an argument. The interpretive representation shouldn't assume that every relationship is a prerequisite. And the proof-plan representation shouldn't be required to express every feature of the source.

But the proof plan remains a central output, not an optional feature that disappears whenever the source becomes difficult.

## 3. What makes this different for a technical paper and Philosophical Investigations?

The difference needn't be that one has a proof plan and the other doesn't. It can be a difference in how directly the interpretive structure maps onto the inferential structure.

A technical paper often makes that mapping relatively explicit. Its sections may establish assumptions, define a method, present results, compare against alternatives, and draw conclusions. The narrative layer helps explain why these stages occur in this order and what role each plays. The proof plan can then represent the claims and their dependencies in considerable detail.

With Philosophical Investigations, the mapping may be less direct. A remark might introduce an analogy, pursue it, expose its limitations, return to an earlier temptation, and redirect the reader's attention. The intellectual movement may be coherent even when it doesn't culminate in a proposition established by a chain of premises.

But this is not a reason to treat the work as a miscellany. It is a reason to allow the reconstructed structure to contain more than one kind of relationship.

For instance:

- A claim might support another claim.
- An example might illustrate a distinction without proving it.
- A passage might challenge a presupposition.
- A later passage might reframe an earlier question.
- A sequence of remarks might develop a line of thought without yielding a single terminal conclusion.

These relations are not interchangeable, and none should be automatically translated into a prerequisite edge. Yet they can all contribute to a coherent reconstruction of the work.

The crucial point is that coherence need not mean linear deduction. A coherent whole can have a structure of dependencies, contrasts, developments, returns, and transformations. The proof plan should express the inferential part of that whole, while the richer representation explains how the parts belong together.

## 4. The local narrative draft could be the bridge

I think your Cliff Notes analogy identifies a particularly useful intermediate representation.

A detailed enumeration of claims is often too close to the source to expose the larger movement. A very general summary is too far away to preserve the evidence. A local narrative draft can occupy the middle ground:

> The author begins with an apparently natural account of the phenomenon, introduces cases that make this account seem plausible, and then develops complications that reveal what the account leaves unexplained. The passage redirects attention from the initial proposed explanation towards a distinction that the subsequent discussion can exploit.

That is a hypothetical example, not a proposed interpretation of a specific passage. Notice what it captures: the starting point, the role of the examples, the pressure on the initial view, and the resulting change in direction.

From that draft, Exegenius could identify questions for the more formal reconstruction:

- What exactly is the initial account?
- Which examples are supposed to support it, and which put pressure on it?
- What, precisely, do the complications establish?
- Does the passage establish the subsequent distinction, merely motivate it, or leave it open?
- Which later claims depend on this development?

The narrative doesn't answer all those questions. It helps identify which questions matter.

And the reverse direction matters too. If the extracted claims don't substantiate the narrative, the narrative may be overinterpreting the source. If the narrative says the author undermines a position but the formal reconstruction only contains positive supporting evidence, something needs to be reconciled.

That gives the two representations a meaningful relationship without forcing either to replace the other.

## 5. Would an unproof-like intermediate representation harm proof-plan extraction?

It could, if “unproof-like” meant replacing the source's claims and evidence with broad summaries of what the author supposedly intended.

A narrative such as “the author explores the limitations of this concept” is not enough to recover a proof plan. It doesn't identify the relevant propositions, distinguish evidence from conclusion, or specify which inference is licensed.

But that isn't the design I have in mind. The intermediate representation would be richer than the proof plan, not less rigorous about evidence.

A useful local draft would have at least two kinds of content:

- A narrative account: the intellectual or argumentative movement of the passage.
- Anchored structure: the claims, examples, assumptions, questions, and other source features that justify that account.

The narrative can be concise, while the anchored structure preserves detail. The proof plan can then be derived from the anchored structure, using the narrative as interpretive guidance rather than as a substitute for evidence.

That is especially useful because a proof plan is itself a selective representation. It has to decide which claims matter, what each step accomplishes, and how the steps fit together. Those are interpretive decisions, even in a technical paper.

So I wouldn't expect the richer intermediate representation to impair proof-plan extraction in principle. It offers a way to make those decisions better informed and more accountable. There is still a design risk that an early narrative could bias the later extraction, which is why the source evidence needs to remain independently accessible and the narrative must be revisable.

## 6. I wouldn't let the 19/19 ceiling decide this question

The 19/19 result and the model-effort comparisons answer a narrower question: whether particular configurations were good enough at the tested task under the tested conditions. They don't establish that the task decomposition is right for every kind of source.

That doesn't diminish the result. It tells you that model choice and effort matter, and that the existing approach can perform well on at least the material you tested. Your observation that Haiku was underpowered, while stronger configurations were good enough, is relevant to implementation choices.

But there are two different uncertainties here:

- Execution uncertainty: given the current task and representation, can the model perform it faithfully?
- Representation uncertainty: does the task and representation capture the structure you actually want to reconstruct?

The experiments mostly inform the first. Your current design question concerns the second. A perfect score on a finite extraction task would not settle it.

Nor does it follow that you need to replace the program's central purpose. You can retain the proof-plan goal while improving the intermediate representations from which plans are produced.

## 7. The design principle I'd preserve

I would formulate the principle this way:

Exegenius reconstructs the coherent intellectual structure of a source and produces a proof-like plan of that structure, preserving the distinction between what the source states, what its parts are doing, and what can be inferred from them.

This has several consequences.

First, the source is not assumed to be a proof in advance. Second, the system is not permitted to stop at a generic narrative summary when a more precise reconstruction is possible. Third, the proof plan is not required to flatten every meaningful relationship into a logical dependency. And finally, the system should preserve the evidence that lets a reader assess whether the reconstruction is faithful.

The goal remains ambitious: not merely to summarise a document, but to recover its structure of thought. A proof plan is one important expression of that structure, not a universal template that every passage must be forced into.

That seems to me a stable direction for the project, rather than a retreat from its original premise.
