Yes — I think the danger is that we have a good intuition about the missing layer, but haven't yet defined it tightly enough to make it part of the program. Naming a new layer and giving it an appealing description is not the same as having a sound design for it.

And I agree that “reconstructs the coherent intellectual structure of a source” is poor as a project description. It's abstract, cumbersome, and doesn't say what Exegenius actually does.

I'd separate two questions.

What is Exegenius for?

I'd keep the original commitment: to turn a source into a structured, evidence-grounded plan of its reasoning. That retains the proof-plan objective without claiming that every source is a formal proof.

What intermediate representations does it need to do that well?

That's the open design question. The content/narrative/argument distinction is promising, but it isn't yet a specification. In particular, we haven't established whether narrative should be a first-class artefact, a field attached to each local analysis, or something derived during planning. Nor have we established how to prevent a narrative from introducing interpretations that the source evidence doesn't warrant.

I wouldn't settle those decisions just because the three-part distinction sounds right.

## How to avoid winging it

I'd want the design to rest on three things before committing to a new representation:

1. A defined purpose. What information does the proposed layer preserve that the current concept graph and proof plan fail to preserve?
2. A defined relationship to existing artefacts. What consumes it, what produces it, and what constraints prevent it from silently overriding the source?
3. A defined failure criterion. What would count as a narrative that is fluent but misleading, or a proof plan that is logically tidy but misrepresents the author's argument?

These are design questions, not a request to run more experiments. They can be discussed against the existing code and its current artefacts.

One distinction I'd especially preserve is between a useful way to describe a passage and a representation the program needs to store. A narrative draft might be excellent for helping a model understand a passage without necessarily deserving its own permanent data structure. Conversely, if that draft captures information that would otherwise be lost, it may merit a formal place in the pipeline.

My recommendation is to treat the narrative layer as a design hypothesis, not a settled architectural decision. Keep the proof-plan objective fixed while investigating whether the existing machinery needs this intermediate representation, and what its contract would have to be.

That way, the project can become more capable of handling Philosophical Investigations without its architecture turning into a collection of intuitively appealing structures whose necessity and relationship to one another remain unclear.
