I think this is quite feasible, and Philosophical Investigations is an especially interesting test case. The key distinction is between the amount of text an LLM can ingest and the amount of reasoning it can reliably decompose, preserve, and verify.

A document can fit comfortably inside a model's context window while still being too conceptually complex to turn into a trustworthy proof plan in one pass. Conversely, a very long work can be processed reliably if it is decomposed into smaller units and its dependencies are tracked explicitly.

I'd distinguish three problems:

1. Extraction: identify the claims, distinctions, premises, objections, examples, and conclusions already present in the work.
2. Decomposition: turn these into a directed graph of smaller argumentative obligations, with explicit dependencies.
3. Verification: check that each inference is justified, that the original argument hasn't been distorted, and that the subarguments collectively support the intended conclusion.

The third is the difficult part. Producing something that looks like a proof plan is relatively easy; producing one that faithfully captures a complicated philosophical argument is much harder.

## 1. What size document are we talking about?

The interesting answer is that there probably isn't a single meaningful size limit. There are several different thresholds.

| Scale         | Likely task                                              | Main difficulty                   |
| ------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| 5–20 pages    | Extract claims and build a proof tree                    | Local interpretation              |
| 20–100 pages  | Construct a structured argument map                      | Keeping distinctions consistent   |
| 100–500 pages | Build a hierarchical plan, section by section            | Cross-references and dependencies |
| 500+ pages    | Build a persistent, incrementally refined argument graph | Global coherence and verification |

These are rough engineering categories, not measured capability limits. A dense 20-page philosophical text might be harder than a 200-page textbook. A formal mathematical paper may be easier to decompose than a shorter work whose central concepts shift subtly throughout.

For context, the research on long-context models has demonstrated that simply fitting more text into a context window does not guarantee that the model will use all of it equally well. Models can underperform when relevant information occurs in the middle of a long input.&#x20;

Philosophical Investigations is a fascinating example because it has many of the properties that make argument reconstruction difficult:

- It develops ideas across numerous numbered remarks rather than following a conventional theorem-proof structure.
- It uses examples, thought experiments, objections, analogies, and changes of perspective.
- It revisits concepts such as meaning, rule-following, understanding, and private experience from different angles.
- Its argumentative force often depends on what a particular example reveals, rather than on an explicit premise followed by a formal deduction.

That last point matters enormously. Treating every remark as a premise in a single deductive argument would risk misrepresenting the work.

Instead, I would represent it as a proof plan in the broader sense of a structured philosophical justification. Some nodes would be deductive arguments; others would be conceptual analyses, counterexamples, challenges to an assumption, or demonstrations that a particular picture of language is misleading.

## 3. What the decomposition would actually look like

Consider the discussion of rule-following. The following is a schematic reconstruction of the argumentative structure, not a claim that Wittgenstein presents this exact formal proof.

### Target claim

What determines whether an application of a rule is correct?

A. The tempting picture

A rule contains, or uniquely determines, all its future applications.

B. The challenge

Any finite formulation appears compatible with multiple possible continuations unless we explain how it is applied.

C. Examine the proposed solution

If another interpretation is invoked to fix the first, what fixes the interpretation? Does this generate a regress, or does it misunderstand how rule-following works?

D. Reconstruct the positive account

Investigate the role of training, shared practices, established criteria, and agreement in action in explaining what counts as following a rule.

The value of the method is not merely that it summarises Wittgenstein. It forces us to identify precisely where an argument depends on an assumption, where an objection applies, and what remains to be established.

A rigorous version would attach to every node:

- The relevant numbered remarks and exact quotations.
- The proposition or question being addressed.
- Its role: premise, conclusion, objection, example, or conceptual clarification.
- The inference or argumentative relation to neighbouring nodes.
- Alternative interpretations and unresolved ambiguities.
- A confidence rating for the reconstruction, distinct from confidence that the original text supports it.

It would also preserve negative results: places where the text does not establish a claim, where the inference is contested, or where two interpretations remain viable.

## 4. Does training on the work give the LLM an advantage?

Yes, potentially—but not quite in the way one might initially imagine.

Suppose a model has encountered Philosophical Investigations during training. It might already have learned associations between its central arguments, standard scholarly interpretations, relevant passages, and familiar philosophical objections.

This can help in three ways:

1. Recognition. The model can recognise the philosophical problem being addressed without having to derive its significance from scratch.
2. Compression. It can use an existing conceptual understanding to organise a large text into a smaller number of meaningful argumentative units.
3. Disambiguation. It can recognise where a passage belongs to a familiar debate and identify plausible alternative interpretations.

But there is a crucial caveat: familiarity is not verification.

A model might reconstruct the argument according to a standard scholarly interpretation rather than what the text actually says. It might import an argument from secondary literature, silently bridge a gap, or attribute a conclusion to Wittgenstein that he deliberately leaves unsettled.

In fact, knowing the work well could make these errors harder to detect, because the reconstruction might be exceptionally plausible.

The ideal system would combine its learned background knowledge with passage-level evidence, explicitly distinguishing:

- What the text states.
- What the reconstruction infers.
- What the scholarly tradition suggests.
- What remains disputed.

## 5. The architecture I'd use

I would not ask the model to produce one enormous proof plan and trust the output. I'd build a system that incrementally constructs and checks an argument graph.

### 01

Global orientation

Read the complete work or its available representation; identify major themes, structure, and recurring concepts.

### 02

Local decomposition

Analyse each passage or small group of remarks, extracting claims, examples, objections, and argumentative roles.

### 03

Argument graph

Connect the local units using explicit relations: supports, challenges, qualifies, presupposes, or illustrates.

### 04

Global reconciliation

Resolve inconsistent terminology, identify dependencies across distant passages, and detect missing inferential steps.

### 05

Adversarial verification

Ask a separate pass to challenge every inference, find counterexamples, and compare the plan against the source.

### 06

Refinement

Revisit the original passages when a conflict or gap is detected, updating the graph without losing provenance.

The most important design decision is that the argument graph should be a persistent data structure, not just prose in a conversation. Each node should have a stable identifier, links to its source passages, explicit dependencies, and a status such as supported, contested, or unresolved.

This gives you something that can be audited, updated, searched, and independently checked. It also makes the overall document size much less important: the model only needs to reconsider the affected parts of the graph when a local interpretation changes.

## 6. How would you know whether it worked?

I'd test it against a few different kinds of text:

- A conventional mathematical proof, where the target is formal validity.
- A philosophical argument with an identifiable conclusion, where premises and inferences can be debated.
- Philosophical Investigations, where even the right representation of the argumentative structure is contested.

The evaluation should measure more than summary quality. It should test whether the reconstruction preserves source attribution, captures counterarguments, identifies unsupported inferential steps, and remains consistent when distant passages are considered together.

For the mathematical case, formalisation in a proof assistant can provide an independent validity check for suitably formalised claims. For Wittgenstein, there is no equivalent universal verifier: the system needs source-grounded reconstruction and expert or scholarly evaluation.

## My bottom line

I suspect that the bottleneck is not document length so much as the complexity of the dependency structure and the availability of a reliable verification procedure.

A single coherent book could be a perfectly reasonable unit of analysis, even if it is hundreds of pages long. The model does not need to hold every detail in active attention simultaneously if it has a hierarchical representation and can retrieve the underlying passages when necessary.

And your point about training is important. If the model already has a useful internal representation of the work, it may be able to construct the initial graph much more efficiently than a model encountering the text without that background. But the real achievement would be to turn that implicit understanding into an explicit, inspectable structure.

One further distinction is worth making: for Wittgenstein, I would aim to produce a map of argumentative obligations, not necessarily a single proof. Some philosophical works are best understood as networks of interlocking challenges rather than as attempts to prove one master proposition.

That is a tractable and interesting problem—and a good test of whether an LLM can turn broad conceptual understanding into a precise, auditable chain of reasoning.
