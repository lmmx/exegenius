## Three experiments I'd prioritise

1\. Separate model capability from pipeline capability.

Run the same fixed source and evaluation questions through your best configuration and a cheaper configuration. Keep the source, reader profile, and evaluation set constant. Measure not just whether the wiki looks good, but whether readers can answer held-out questions that require combining information across multiple pages.

Your Sonnet High results are encouraging, but the most valuable result would be knowing which stages genuinely benefit from more reasoning effort and which don't.

2\. Test a work whose interpretation is the challenge.

Your OpenAI paper is a good technical benchmark because mathematical dependencies give you relatively concrete criteria for judging the reconstruction. Philosophical Investigations tests a different capability: whether the system can faithfully reconstruct an argument without inventing a more conventional one.

I'd start with a bounded section, then compare two independently generated argument plans. Have a separate evaluator identify where they disagree about premises, inferential relationships, or the status of a conclusion. Those disagreements are potentially more informative than an overall quality score.

3\. Measure convergence, not just the final artefact.

Exegenius already tracks gaps between audit rounds and stops when improvements cease to meet its convergence criterion.&#x20;

## One larger thought

I suspect the grand challenge is eventually to build something like an executable specification of an intellectual work.

For mathematics, that might mean a proof plan whose individual steps can ultimately be formalised and checked.

For philosophy, it would mean a graph of claims, reasons, objections, conceptual dependencies, and interpretive choices, with every edge traceable to the source and every contested inference explicitly represented.

The ambitious part is that these could be two instances of the same underlying system. The difference would be the strength of the verification available, not the basic architecture.

And there is a particularly interesting possibility here: the system could become better at reconstructing a work by discovering where its own reconstruction fails. The reader simulation is one mechanism for doing that; independent argument reconstruction is another.

One question I'd be especially interested in: in your swarm experiments, have you found that increasing the model's reasoning effort improves the actual argument structure, or does most of the gain come from better local explanations, more careful source attribution, and fewer missed dependencies? That distinction would tell us a great deal about where the real bottleneck lies.
