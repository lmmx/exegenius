Cutting each input into digits in a power-of-two base turns the integer product into a product of polynomials, whose coefficient list is a convolution of the digit lists. This page checks that a cyclic convolution of the right length gives that polynomial product exactly, and that its coefficients stay small.

## Digits as polynomials

Let $n$ be the number of bits of each input, and $b$ the number of bits in one digit, with $b=\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$ (see [Parameter choices and prime source lengths](parameter-choice.md)). Write the two inputs as polynomials in $2^b$, each with $q=\lceil n/b\rceil$ nonnegative digits, where $q$ is the number of digits per input. For example, with $b=3$ the 8-bit number $181=10\,110\,101_2$ has $q=3$ digits $5,6,2$ and equals $5+6\cdot 8+2\cdot 8^2$.

If $A(x)$ and $B(x)$ have the digits of the two inputs as coefficients, the inputs are $A(2^b)$ and $B(2^b)$, so their product is $C(2^b)$ with $C=AB$. The coefficients $c_j$ of $C$ may exceed $2^b$; carries, handled in [Rounding and carry propagation](exact-recovery.md), repair this. The integer product is therefore computed as a [Cyclic convolution](cyclic-convolution.md) of the two digit lists, zero-padded to the cyclic length defined below.

## No wraparound

Here $S$ is the cyclic length, a product of distinct primes chosen so that $T/2<S<T$, where $T$ is the power of two with $4n/b\le T<8n/b$ that sizes the padded array. Convolution modulo $x^S-1$ replaces each $x^j$ by $x^{j \bmod S}$, so it leaves $C$ unchanged exactly when $\deg C<S$.

Each of $A,B$ has degree at most $q-1$, so $C$ has degree at most $2q-2$. Since $q<n/b+1$, we get $2q-2<2n/b$. Since $T\ge 4n/b$, we get $2n/b\le T/2$, and $T/2<S$. Thus
$$2q-2<2n/b\le T/2<S.$$
Every exponent of $C$ is below $S$, so reduction modulo $x^S-1$ changes nothing: the cyclic convolution has no nonzero wraparound <cite data-source="paper">Thus convolution modulo $x^S-1$ has no nonzero wraparound.</cite>

## Size of the coefficients

Each $c_j=\sum_k a_k b_{j-k}$ is a sum of at most $q$ products of digits, and each digit lies between $0$ and $2^b-1$. Hence
$$0\le c_j\le q(2^b-1)^2<2^{3b}.$$
The last step uses $(2^b-1)^2<2^{2b}$ and $q\le n\le2^b$: $q\le n$ because $b\ge1$, and $n\le2^b$ because $b=\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$. So each coefficient fits in $3b$ bits <cite data-source="paper">0\le c_j\le q(2^b-1)^2<2^{3b},</cite>
